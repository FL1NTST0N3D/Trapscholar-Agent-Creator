# Skill: Positioning Analysis (`/comp positioning`)

## Purpose
Build perceptual positioning maps that visualize where competitors sit relative to each other and to the user's own offering. Identify whitespace opportunities, crowded zones, and potential for category creation. Positioning analysis transforms subjective market perception into a structured visual framework that drives strategic decisions about where to compete and how to differentiate.

## When to Use
- Defining or refining your market positioning strategy
- Launching a new product and choosing where to position it
- Evaluating whether to compete head-on or reposition into whitespace
- Preparing investor or board materials that show market landscape
- Sales team needs clear "why us" language relative to alternatives
- Considering category creation vs competing in an existing category

## Framework: Perceptual Positioning Maps

### Step 1: Select Positioning Dimensions
Choose two axes that represent the most meaningful differentiators in the market. Common dimension pairs:

**For SaaS / Software:**
- Price vs Feature Depth
- Ease of Use vs Power/Flexibility
- SMB Focus vs Enterprise Focus
- Point Solution vs Platform
- Self-Serve vs Sales-Led
- Vertical-Specific vs Horizontal

**For Services / Agencies:**
- Price vs Quality/Expertise
- Specialization vs Full-Service
- Strategic vs Tactical
- Local vs Global Reach
- Boutique vs Scale

**For E-commerce / DTC:**
- Price vs Perceived Quality
- Mass Market vs Niche/Premium
- Functional vs Aspirational
- Convenience vs Experience

**Custom Dimensions**: Ask the user what their buyers actually evaluate when comparing options. The best positioning maps use dimensions that map to real purchase criteria.

### Step 2: Map Competitors
For each competitor, assign a position on both axes using a 1-10 scale:

```
| Competitor | [Axis 1] (1-10) | [Axis 2] (1-10) | Rationale |
|------------|------------------|------------------|-----------|
| Competitor A | 8 | 3 | High price, low complexity |
| Competitor B | 4 | 7 | Mid price, high complexity |
| Your Company | ? | ? | To be determined |
```

### Step 3: Build the 2x2 Matrix
Divide the map into four quadrants and label each:

```
                    High [Axis 2]
                         |
          Q2             |            Q1
    [Label: e.g.,        |     [Label: e.g.,
     "Premium            |      "Premium
      Simple"]           |       Complex"]
                         |
    ─────────────────────┼─────────────────────
                         |
          Q3             |            Q4
    [Label: e.g.,        |     [Label: e.g.,
     "Budget             |      "Budget
      Simple"]           |       Complex"]
                         |
                    Low [Axis 2]
```

### Step 4: Identify Patterns
Analyze the completed map for:

- **Clusters**: Where are competitors bunched together? These are red ocean zones with intense competition and commoditization pressure.
- **Whitespace**: Which quadrants are empty or underpopulated? These represent potential blue ocean opportunities, but validate that whitespace exists for a reason (maybe there's no demand there).
- **Movement Vectors**: Which direction are competitors trending? A competitor investing in enterprise features is moving up the complexity axis.
- **Your Position**: Where do you currently sit, and is that where you want to be?

### Step 5: Strategic Recommendations

**Option A — Compete Head-On**: Position near the cluster and differentiate on execution, brand, or a specific feature advantage. Best when you have resource parity and a genuine edge.

**Option B — Reposition into Whitespace**: Move to an underserved quadrant. Best when there's validated demand and you can credibly deliver in that position.

**Option C — Create a New Category**: Define new axes entirely. Instead of competing on existing dimensions, change the conversation. Best when current category definitions don't capture your true differentiation.

**Option D — Niche Down**: Pick a vertical or use case and own it completely. Move off the general map into a specialized sub-market where you can be the default choice.

## Multi-Map Analysis
For comprehensive positioning, build multiple maps using different dimension pairs:

1. **Buyer Perception Map**: How customers see the market (ease of use vs cost)
2. **Product Reality Map**: Objective feature/capability comparison
3. **Brand Perception Map**: Market awareness vs brand sentiment
4. **Trajectory Map**: Current position vs projected position in 12-18 months

The gaps between perception maps and reality maps reveal messaging opportunities.

## Best Practices
1. Always validate dimension choices with actual buyer research or review analysis — do not assume what buyers care about
2. Plot at least 5-7 competitors for a meaningful map; fewer than 4 makes pattern detection unreliable
3. Use multiple maps with different dimension pairs to get a 360-degree view
4. Remember that whitespace is not always opportunity — sometimes a quadrant is empty because there is no demand there
5. Positioning is about perception, not just product reality; the gap between the two is your messaging challenge
6. Revisit positioning maps quarterly as competitors shift and your own product evolves
