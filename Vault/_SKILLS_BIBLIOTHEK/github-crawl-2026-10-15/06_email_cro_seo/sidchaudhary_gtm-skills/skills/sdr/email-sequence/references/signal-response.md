# Signal Response

For any skill that turns an observed event into an action: ranking a week's signals, writing the
touch that follows one, or deciding whether a signal is worth acting on at all.

The core claim, and the one most signal-based outreach gets wrong: **the signal is never the opener.**
It is the context that makes a message relevant, not the message. Stating it back to someone tells
them what they already know happened to them, and reads as surveillance rather than relevance.

---

## 1. Signal → Pain → Bridge → Ask

Four steps. The signal does its work in step 1 and then gets out of the way.

| Step | What it does | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | The observable event. Used as context, **not stated** | A VP of Sales started a new role three weeks ago |
| **Pain** | What the event changed *for them*: the pressure it created, the promises they made to get there, the problems they inherited | They committed to a 30/60/90 to win the job. They inherited a team they did not hire. They need early pipeline to validate the decision to hire them. |
| **Bridge** | Connect that new reality to what you actually sell. Specific to your product, not generic advice | The link between the pressure in step 2 and the thing you do about it |
| **Ask** | A next step sized to the bandwidth their situation actually leaves them | Someone three weeks into a VP role has very little |

**The amateur version of every one of these is "congrats on the new role."** It states the signal,
adds nothing, and spends the opening line proving you can read a job-change alert.

## 2. Signal → pain, worked

The pattern is always the same: what pressure did this create, and what did they promise or inherit?

| Signal | The pain it actually creates |
|---|---|
| New VP of Sales or Head of Growth | Committed to a plan to get the job. Inherited a team and a stack they did not choose. Everything is under review in the first 90 days. |
| Job change to a new company | Blank slate on tooling. Old vendor relationships gone. In evaluation mode whether or not they say so. |
| Promotion to Director or above | More visibility, a number that is now board-facing, and a promotion to justify. |
| Raised a round | Took the money against a growth mandate. Every hire, tool and campaign is now under board scrutiny. The pressure moved from "can we grow" to "can we grow efficiently." |
| Hiring for a function they did not have | Something broke badly enough that headcount was approved to fix it. The new hire is expected to fix it fast. |
| Hiring reps | Building the function from scratch. New reps need ramp time, and pipeline is needed *during* the ramp, not after. |
| Engaging with a competitor's content | Actively comparing. If nobody reaches them during the comparison, someone else gets chosen by default. |
| Posted publicly about a problem you solve | Admitted in public that the current approach is not working. Under pressure and looking for answers. |
| Good quarter | Budget and momentum, plus a board asking what is next. |
| A champion going quiet | Something changed: busy, frustrated, or evaluating. Silence is the loudest churn signal there is. |

## 3. Signals that inform the message and never appear in it

Some events are useful intelligence and unusable as an opener. Using them is not just ineffective, it
is the kind of thing that ends a relationship before it starts.

**Never lead with, never mention:**

- **Layoffs or a restructure.** Shape the message around doing more with less. Naming it is grotesque.
- **A bad quarter or a missed number.** Shape the message around efficiency and scrutiny. Naming it
  is a jab.
- **A profile view.** They already know they looked. Mentioning it makes the exchange strange, and
  the opener is already written for you: reach out about the thing that actually caught your eye.
- **Anything from a private or internal source.** A competitor mentioned on a call, a support ticket,
  a usage drop, a data-export request. These are yours to act on, not to reveal you have.
- **Personal circumstances of any kind.** Even publicly posted.

The test: if naming the signal would make the recipient wonder how closely they are being watched,
use it to shape the message and leave it out of the text.

## 4. Weight signals by decay, level, and confirmation

**Decay is real and steep.** A funding round from six months ago means nothing; a job change from two
weeks ago is the strongest thing on the list. Every signal has a window in which acting on it makes
sense, and outside that window it is trivia. Record the signal's **date**, not just its type, and
rank on freshness alongside strength.

**Person-level beats account-level.** Knowing that a specific named person just changed jobs is worth
more than knowing a 500-person company is "showing intent," because you know who to write to and what
changed for them. Account-level intent tells you a building is warm.

**Combination beats any single signal.** One signal rarely converts on its own. A funding round *and*
an open req for the function you serve *and* a pricing-page visit is a buying window; any one of them
alone is a maybe. Where two or more independent signals converge on the same account in the same
window, say so and rank it above anything single-sourced.

**Speed is part of the signal.** A strong signal acted on within hours converts; the same signal a
few days later often does not, because the window it opened has closed or someone else walked through
it. If the operating cadence cannot act inside a signal's window, that signal is not worth monitoring
at that cadence, say so rather than collecting it.

## 5. Precision over volume

Five signals that reliably mean something beat fifty that mostly do not. Every false positive spends
some of the operator's trust in the whole system, and once they start skimming the list, the true
positives go with it.

- **A signal without a stated reason is noise.** Every item needs why it matters *for this account*
  and what to do about it, or it is a row someone has to re-derive.
- **Do not automate a signal until it has been shown to convert.** Run new signals as alerts with
  human review first. Automating a signal that turns out to be noise scales the noise.
- **Drop signals that never lead to action.** A source that has produced nothing actionable in a
  month is costing attention. Say so and cut it rather than leaving it in the stack.

## 6. Check before returning

1. Does the recommended touch lead with the **pain**, not the signal, so nothing states back to the
   person what they already know happened to them?
2. Is the pain specific to what the event changed for *them*, the pressure, the promise, the
   inherited problem, rather than a generic category pain?
3. Does the bridge connect to what the user actually sells, rather than being generic advice that
   would fit any vendor?
4. Is the ask sized to the bandwidth their situation leaves them?
5. Is every sensitive signal (layoffs, missed numbers, profile views, internal or private data,
   personal circumstances) used only to shape the message and absent from the text?
6. Does every signal carry its **date**, with ranking accounting for decay rather than type alone?
7. Are converging signals on one account identified and ranked above single-sourced ones?
8. Is person-level signal distinguished from account-level, rather than blended into one score?
9. Does every item state why it matters for this account and what to do next?
10. Where the operating cadence cannot act inside a signal's window, is that said rather than the
    signal being collected anyway?
