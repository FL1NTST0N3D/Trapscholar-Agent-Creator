# Referral incentive benchmarks

## Incentive structures by pricing model

**Low-ACV subscription (under $50/mo):** cash or account credit rarely clears the bar for effort. A free month or a permanent small discount (10-20%) for both sides tends to outperform a one-time cash reward, because it reinforces continued use rather than paying for a single action.

**Mid-ACV (roughly $50-500/mo):** a fixed account credit sized at 1-2 months of the referrer's own plan value is the common range. Two-sided (advocate and referred friend both get something) consistently outperforms one-sided in willingness to refer, even when the total cost to the business is similar.

**High-ACV / enterprise:** cash or gift-card incentives at this tier usually violate procurement or gifts-and-entertainment policy for the referring contact's company. Non-cash recognition (co-marketing, case study placement, early access to features, advisory board access) works better and avoids putting the advocate in an awkward compliance position.

## Trigger timing

Referral asks made within the first 14 days after a clear win (a milestone hit, a positive support resolution, a successful renewal) convert meaningfully better than asks made on a fixed calendar cadence disconnected from any event. The weakest-performing pattern is a generic "refer a friend" link surfaced in a footer or a static account settings page with no triggering moment at all.

## Anti-abuse patterns by incentive type

- **Cash or gift card rewards:** require the referred account to convert to paying and stay active for a minimum period (30-90 days is typical) before payout. Without this, self-referral and rapid signup-cancel cycling is the most common abuse pattern.
- **Account credit:** cap the number of stacked referral credits active at once, or credits alone can eliminate a customer's bill entirely, which usually isn't the intended economics.
- **Free upgrades/tier credit:** time-box the upgrade rather than making it permanent, so the incentive has a renewing reason to keep referring rather than a one-time cliff.

## The metric that actually matters

Referral link shares and unique visits are vanity signals - they measure exposure, not effect. Referral-to-paying-customer conversion rate is the number that reflects whether the program is generating real growth versus generating noise. A program with low share volume but a high conversion rate on the shares it does get is healthier than the reverse.

## Legal Constraints on Incentives

A referral incentive is a payment for a recommendation, which puts a referral programme inside
several regimes at once. Most of these are cheap to comply with and expensive to discover late.

| Constraint | What it requires | When it bites |
|---|---|---|
| **Disclosure of a material connection** | An advocate who recommends publicly while receiving a reward has to disclose that they were incentivised, clearly and near the recommendation | Any programme where advocates post on social, review sites, or communities. The obligation sits with the brand to instruct and monitor, not only with the advocate. |
| **Prize draws and sweepstakes law** | Randomised rewards ("refer a friend, enter to win") are regulated, frequently require published rules, eligibility terms, and an odds statement, and can be unlawful where a purchase is a condition of entry | The moment the reward is a chance rather than a certainty |
| **Tax reporting** | Cash and cash-equivalent rewards can be reportable income above jurisdictional thresholds, and may require collecting details from the advocate | Cash, gift cards, and prepaid cards. Account credit against the advocate's own subscription is usually treated differently. |
| **Referral-fee and anti-kickback rules** | In regulated sectors, healthcare, financial services, legal, insurance, and others, paying for a customer referral is restricted or prohibited outright, sometimes criminally | Any programme in a regulated industry. This is a stop, not a design constraint. |
| **The advocate's own employer policy** | Enterprise and B2B advocates are often barred by gifts-and-entertainment rules from accepting anything cash-like | B2B programmes. A charity donation or a product credit is frequently the only acceptable form. |
| **Platform terms** | Review sites generally prohibit incentivised reviews outright, whatever the disclosure | Anything that rewards leaving a review rather than making an introduction |

Rules that follow:

- **Never incentivise a review.** Incentivise an introduction. Rewarding reviews breaches most
  review platforms' terms and is separately treated as deceptive in many jurisdictions, even
  disclosed.
- **Prefer a certain reward to a chance one.** A fixed reward avoids sweepstakes law entirely, and
  converts better.
- **In a regulated sector, stop and say so.** Where paying for referrals may be prohibited, the
  honest output is that the programme needs a compliance review before design, with the non-monetary
  alternatives named (recognition, early access, community status, a donation).
- **Write the disclosure instruction into the programme**, not just the terms. Advocates will not
  invent it, and the brand carries the exposure.

Specifics vary by jurisdiction and sector, and this is guidance for framing rather than legal
advice. A programme with real budget behind it needs a real review.

## Did the Referral Actually Add a Customer

Two questions decide whether a referral programme makes money, and neither is answered by the
referral conversion rate.

### Do referred customers stay

An incentive attracts two populations: people who genuinely recommend, and people motivated by the
reward. The second group brings customers who signed up for the discount, and they churn at a
different rate.

- Track the referred cohort's retention **separately** from organic, at the same intervals used
  everywhere else. A referral programme that acquires cheaply and churns fast is a discount
  programme with extra steps.
- Compare **contribution over the retained period**, not signups. Referred customer count is the
  vanity number one level up from links shared.
- If referred retention runs materially below organic, the incentive is too large or aimed at the
  wrong moment. Reducing the reward usually improves cohort quality, which is the opposite of the
  instinct when a programme underperforms.

### Would they have converted anyway

A referral bounty paid on a customer who was already arriving is pure cost, and last-touch
attribution reports it as a win.

- Watch for the pattern where the referral code is applied at checkout by someone who found the
  product independently. A code that circulates publicly stops being a referral mechanism and
  becomes an unmanaged discount.
- **Keep codes non-public and per-advocate.** A code that can be posted to a deals site will be.
- Where volume allows, hold out a share of the eligible advocate pool and compare acquisition
  between them. Without a holdout, the programme's incremental effect is an assumption rather than a
  measurement, and it should be labelled as one.
- Set the review point when the programme is designed: the date at which referred-cohort retention
  and incremental acquisition get checked against the reward cost, and what result would mean
  changing or ending it.

## Referral Benchmarks, and Where the Constraint Actually Is

| Measure | Benchmark |
|---|---|
| Satisfied customers **willing** to refer | ~83% |
| Satisfied customers who **actually do** | ~29% |
| Programme participation rate | ~12-15% typical; above 25% is exceptional |
| Referral conversion rate (e-commerce) | ~3-5% median; 8%+ top quartile |
| Referred-customer conversion vs other channels | 3-5x higher |
| Referred-customer lifetime value | ~16% higher (Wharton) |

### Willingness is not the constraint

The gap between **83% willing** and **29% doing** is the single most useful number here. It says the
limiting factor is almost never that customers are unwilling, it is that **nobody asked them at a
moment when acting was easy.**

That reorders the design work:

1. **The trigger moment**, asking at a point of felt satisfaction, not on a schedule
2. **The friction of the ask**, how many steps between wanting to refer and having referred
3. **The incentive**, last, and usually the least of the three

A programme underperforming at 5% participation is far more likely to have a timing or friction problem
than an incentive problem, so **raising the reward is the wrong first move.** It also degrades cohort
quality, per the Did the Referral Add a Customer section: a larger reward pulls in reward-motivated
signups who churn differently.

### Two-sided incentives, honestly reported

Two-sided rewards do outperform one-sided, but the published effect sizes vary widely: reported lifts on
participation range from about **+29% to +91%** depending on the study, with completion rates around
**52% two-sided against 29% one-sided**. About **78%** of programmes use a two-sided structure.

Use the direction, not the number. Two-sided is the right default because it gives the advocate something
to say that is not purely self-interested, "you get X too" is easier to send than "I get X if you sign
up." Do not quote a specific lift to the user as though it were a forecast; the spread across studies is
too wide to promise.

### Set expectations before designing

Tell the user the realistic band up front: **12-15% participation and 3-5% referral conversion** are
normal, and 25%+ participation is exceptional rather than a target. A programme judged against an
imagined 50% will be called a failure while performing at the benchmark, and then get its incentive
raised for no reason.
