# Worked finding examples — B2B homepage quality calibration

These examples show what a high-quality B2B homepage finding looks like across different B2B types and ICE scores. Use them as a reference standard.

---

## Example 1: 🔴 Critical — Above-the-fold value proposition & B2B positioning (wholesale fashion B2B)

### 🔴 Above-the-fold — "Welcome to [Brand]" headline says nothing about category or buyer

**Diagnosis**
The homepage opens with "Welcome to [Brand] — your partner in fashion" — failing April Dunford's three positioning tests simultaneously: no category context (fashion could mean retail, wholesale, manufacturing, design), no differentiator (every fashion supplier claims "partner"), no "for who" specificity (boutique retailers? department stores? online resellers?). Per WiderFunnel LIFT's value-proposition factor, the hero must communicate within 5 seconds **what** the company is, **who** it serves, and **what** the visitor's next step is. The current hero fails all three. Per Edelman B2B Trust Barometer, vague positioning fails the verification test that B2B procurement applies during supplier evaluation. A boutique-retail buyer landing here from a wholesale-search query has no anchors to determine whether this supplier serves their segment — typical response is back-button within 8 seconds.

**Recommendation**
Replace the opening with a specific category + customer + differentiator structure. Concrete pattern: "[Brand] supplies premium [specific product category] to [specific customer type] across [region]. [Differentiator]." Example: "Atelier Roest supplies sustainable women's fashion to independent boutiques across NL, BE, and DE. 120+ brands · MOQ from 6 pieces · Net-30 for approved accounts." Pair with a primary CTA matched to the wholesale B2B type: "Apply for trade account" (anonymous) with secondary "Log in" for returning. Add credibility anchor as subhead: "Established 2014 · 340+ active boutique partners · Showroom Amsterdam & Antwerp." Use authentic operational imagery (a boutique buyer reviewing samples in showroom, or fulfillment in warehouse) rather than fashion editorial imagery — operational imagery signals trade supplier, editorial signals consumer brand.

**Test specification**
- **Hypothesis:** If we replace the generic welcome headline with a specific category + customer + differentiator structure, then bounce rate will decrease and click-through to "Apply for trade account" will increase because boutique-retailer prospects immediately understand whether this supplier matches their segment (April Dunford positioning; WiderFunnel LIFT; Edelman B2B Trust).
- **Variant A:** "Welcome to [Brand] — your partner in fashion"
- **Variant B:** Category + customer + region + differentiator headline with credibility subhead
- **Primary metric:** click-through to trade-account application / login
- **Secondary metrics:** bounce rate, time on page, scroll depth, multi-session return
- **Expected impact:** +15% to +30% on trade-account-application click-through
- **ICE:** I=7, C=8, E=10 → 8.3
- **Source:** April Dunford "Obviously Awesome" positioning; WiderFunnel LIFT model; Edelman B2B Trust Barometer 2024-2025

## Example 2: 🔴 Critical — Trade/account program signals & login conversion (wholesale B2B, anonymous visitor)

### 🔴 Trade program — "Apply for trade account" CTA absent; benefits unstated

**Diagnosis**
The homepage shows products with "Log in for pricing" placeholders but provides no visible "Apply for trade account" CTA, no explanation of trade-program benefits, and no approval-timeline expectation. For an anonymous wholesale visitor (first-time prospect evaluating whether to apply), the current state offers no path forward — they can't see pricing, don't know what trade-account benefits look like, and have no clear application process. Per Baymard's B2B account research, anonymous wholesale prospects need clear value framing for trade accounts AND clear expectations for approval timeline. Per MEDDIC's Decision Process axis, the application process IS the decision process for wholesale B2B — making it invisible suppresses the entire prospect funnel. The current homepage works for returning trade customers (who already have accounts) but actively suppresses new-prospect conversion.

**Recommendation**
Add a dedicated trade-program section above the fold or directly below. Concrete elements:
1. **Prominent CTA**: "Apply for trade account" button, filled brand color, alongside secondary "Log in" for returning
2. **Benefits clearly stated** (4-6 specific benefits): "Wholesale pricing (30-50% off retail) · Net-30 payment terms for approved accounts · Dedicated account manager · MOQ from 6 pieces · Free shipping above €X · Quarterly trade events"
3. **Approval timeline transparent**: "Trade accounts approved within 2 business days. Required: business registration, sample order intent, application form (~3 minutes)."
4. **Eligibility clarified**: "We work with independent boutiques, online retailers, and concept stores. Department stores by appointment."

Place the trade-program section above featured products — for anonymous visitors, the application decision is the dominant homepage job. After login (returning trade customers), the section is replaced with personalized homepage content (reorder shortcut, account-specific products, saved lists).

**Test specification**
- **Hypothesis:** If we add a visible trade-program section with benefits, application CTA, and approval-timeline transparency, then anonymous-to-trade-application conversion will increase substantially because prospects gain the clarity needed to commit to the application (Baymard B2B account research; MEDDIC Decision Process axis).
- **Variant A:** No trade-program signals on anonymous homepage
- **Variant B:** Dedicated trade-program section above fold with CTA + benefits + timeline + eligibility
- **Primary metric:** anonymous-to-trade-account-application conversion rate
- **Secondary metrics:** time on homepage, scroll-to-trade-section rate, application form completion rate
- **Expected impact:** +30% to +80% on anonymous-to-application conversion (large because baseline is structurally suppressed)
- **ICE:** I=9, C=8, E=7 → 8.0
- **Source:** Baymard Institute B2B account research; MEDDIC framework (Decision Process)

## Example 3: 🔴 Critical — Trust signals, scale & credibility (transactional industrial supplier)

### 🔴 Trust signals — No scale or credibility signals visible above the fold

**Diagnosis**
The above-the-fold area shows hero imagery, headline, and CTA but contains zero trust signals: no years operating, no customer count, no certifications, no geographic footprint, no industry tenure indication. Per Edelman B2B Trust Barometer, **73% of B2B buyers say trust in the supplier is critical to closing** — and trust signals must be visible early for prospect visitors. Per Cialdini's authority and social proof principles, B2B procurement-led visitors look for verifiable credibility anchors during shortlist evaluation. The current homepage forces a procurement evaluator to scroll significantly to find any credibility signals (located in a small footer strip), failing the 5-second viability check that determines whether the supplier makes the shortlist at all.

**Recommendation**
Add a trust strip directly below the hero (or integrated into the hero subhead) containing 4-6 specific scale signals. Concrete elements:
- "Since 1987 — 37 years in industrial supply"
- "1,847 active business customers across 14 countries"
- "ISO 9001 + ISO 14001 certified"
- "230 employees · 3 European warehouses"
- "97% on-time delivery (rolling 12-month)"

Format as horizontal strip with subtle icons. For mobile, stack vertically or rotate through 2-3 strongest signals. Pair with industry-relevant certification logos (with year + certificate number visible on hover/expand). Specific numbers always outperform rounded numbers — "1,847" beats "1,000+" because it signals that the brand actually counts and reports. Update quarterly or at minimum annually after fiscal-year close.

**Test specification**
- **Hypothesis:** If we add a trust strip with specific scale and credibility signals below the hero, then bounce rate will decrease and downstream conversion will improve because procurement-led visitors immediately get the viability anchors they need to evaluate (Edelman B2B Trust Barometer; Cialdini authority + social proof).
- **Variant A:** No trust signals above the fold (only in footer)
- **Variant B:** Trust strip below hero with 4-6 specific scale + credibility signals
- **Primary metric:** bounce rate (homepage)
- **Secondary metrics:** click-through to product catalog, time on page, multi-session return rate
- **Expected impact:** -5% to -15% on bounce rate; +8% to +15% on click-through
- **ICE:** I=7, C=8, E=9 → 8.0
- **Source:** Edelman B2B Trust Barometer 2024-2025; Cialdini "Influence" (authority + social proof)

## Example 4: 🟠 Important — Customer logos, references & social proof (quote-driven engineering)

### 🟠 Customer logos — Logo wall absent despite 47 active multi-year contracts

**Diagnosis**
The homepage shows no customer logo wall, no named-customer references, and no case-study previews — despite the about-us page indicating 47 active multi-year contracts with engineering customers across offshore wind, heavy lift, and marine industries. Per Cialdini's social proof principle, B2B prospects in evaluation look specifically for "do they work with companies like mine?" — and the absence of recognizable customer signals leaves that question unanswered at the homepage entry point. Per MEDDIC's Champion enablement principle, the B2B champion needs shareable proof for internal advocacy — and a champion exploring this engineering supplier has no immediate proof artifacts to send to procurement.

**Recommendation**
Add a customer logo wall section between trust signals and featured content. Concrete elements:
1. **Logo wall**: 12-18 recognizable customer logos (offshore wind operators, heavy-lift contractors, marine engineering firms), grouped or labeled by industry
2. **Permission documented**: confirm authorized customer-logo usage via standard reference agreements
3. **Named testimonial above logos**: 1 short testimonial with full attribution (name, role, company, photo)
4. **Case-study CTA below logos**: "View case studies →" routing to detail pages with downloadable PDFs

Avoid placing case-study content on the homepage itself — homepage logo wall + testimonial + CTA routing to detail pages performs better than embedded case studies (which compete with other homepage content). For prospects in evaluation, the logo wall is the high-leverage signal; case studies serve deeper-funnel evaluation.

**Test specification**
- **Hypothesis:** If we add a customer logo wall with 12-18 industry-recognized customers, named testimonial, and case-study CTA, then prospect engagement and case-study downloads will increase because B2B champions gain immediate shareable proof for internal advocacy (Cialdini social proof; MEDDIC Champion enablement; Spiegel testimonial research).
- **Variant A:** No customer logos or references on homepage
- **Variant B:** Logo wall + named testimonial + case-study CTA section
- **Primary metric:** click-through to case studies / quote-request rate
- **Secondary metrics:** time on homepage, scroll-to-logo-section rate, case-study download rate
- **Expected impact:** +15% to +30% on case-study CTR; +5% to +12% on quote-request rate
- **ICE:** I=6, C=7, E=6 → 6.3
- **Source:** Cialdini "Influence" (social proof); MEDDIC framework (Champion enablement); Spiegel Research Center

## Example 5: 🟠 Important — Featured categories, products & quick-reorder (transactional B2B, logged-in)

### 🟠 Featured content — No quick-reorder section for logged-in repeat customers

**Diagnosis**
The logged-in homepage shows featured products and category routes identical to the anonymous homepage — no quick-reorder section, no saved-list quick access, no "your last order" reorder shortcut. For a transactional B2B (MRO supplier with repeat-purchase customer base), this is a missed personalization opportunity at the highest-conversion moment of the funnel. Per Baymard's B2B homepage research, account-aware personalization yields 15-30% lifts on logged-in conversion when implemented well. Per Jobs-to-be-Done framework, the logged-in repeat customer's dominant job is reorder — they want fast access to previous purchases, not generic featured content.

**Recommendation**
Add a personalized section to the logged-in homepage (replacing or above current featured products). Concrete elements:
1. **"Reorder your favorites" section**: top 6-8 frequently-ordered SKUs from this customer's order history, with one-click add-to-cart
2. **"Last order" shortcut**: "Reorder your 12 Sept 2024 order (240 units, €1,847)" with one-click reorder
3. **"Saved lists" quick access**: dropdown of named saved lists (e.g., "Engineering quarterly", "Maintenance recurring")
4. **Personalized welcome**: "Welcome back, [First name] · Your account: [Company name]"
5. **Account-specific content**: featured products from categories this customer regularly orders (vs generic featured)

For repeat customers, the personalized section should occupy the position currently held by anonymous-targeted featured content. The trade-program signals (for anonymous visitors) hide automatically when logged in. The result: anonymous and logged-in homepages serve different jobs with the same template.

**Test specification**
- **Hypothesis:** If we add quick-reorder, saved-list, and personalized featured content to the logged-in homepage, then logged-in homepage-to-cart conversion will increase substantially because repeat customers can immediately access their reorder workflow (Baymard B2B homepage research; Jobs-to-be-Done framework).
- **Variant A:** Logged-in homepage identical to anonymous (no personalization)
- **Variant B:** Personalized section with reorder + saved lists + customer-specific featured content
- **Primary metric:** logged-in homepage-to-cart conversion rate
- **Secondary metrics:** time-to-cart, reorder rate, saved-list engagement
- **Expected impact:** +12% to +28% on logged-in homepage-to-cart conversion
- **ICE:** I=7, C=7, E=5 → 6.3
- **Source:** Baymard Institute B2B homepage research; Jobs-to-be-Done framework

## Example 6: 🟢 Nice-to-have — Content marketing & educational routes (transactional, content not core to strategy)

### 🟢 Content section — No featured content section on homepage

**Diagnosis**
The homepage contains no featured content section (no white papers, no case studies, no industry insights). For this transactional B2B (office supplies and MRO), the absence is less critical than for quote-driven or expertise-led B2B — the dominant homepage jobs (catalog browsing, reorder, account management) don't depend on content-led funneling. Per Challenger Sale framework, educational content is valuable but more impactful for expertise-positioned suppliers; for commodity transactional B2B, product and pricing competitiveness dominate. Note: marked Nice-to-have because the business case requires customer-research to validate whether content marketing aligns with brand strategy.

**Recommendation**
Two paths, depending on strategic direction:
1. **If content marketing is part of brand strategy**: add a "Resources" section with 2-3 featured pieces (procurement-related guides, industry pricing insights, sustainability reports). Route from homepage to dedicated /resources page for deeper library.
2. **If content marketing is NOT part of strategy**: do not force it. A homepage without content can be entirely correct for transactional B2B where product-led competition is the strategic focus.

Recommend deciding strategically first: does the brand want to compete on content/expertise OR on product/pricing/service? Adding generic blog posts without strategic commitment underperforms making no claims.

**Test specification**
Test not recommended yet — strategy decision first:
- Decide whether content marketing is part of brand differentiation
- If yes: research what content procurement-led visitors actually engage with
- If no: leave homepage content-free and focus on product/pricing/service signals

**ICE:** I=4, C=5, E=6 → 5.0

**Source:** Challenger Sale (Dixon & Adamson); content-led B2B funnel research

## What makes these examples high-quality

- **B2B-type-aware:** each example signals which B2B type and calibrates recommendations
- **Account-state-explicit:** findings mention whether observation is from anonymous or logged-in screenshots
- **B2B-frameworks integrated:** April Dunford, MEDDIC, Edelman B2B Trust Barometer, Challenger Sale appear alongside core CRO frameworks
- **Specific:** every observation names exact elements, copy, behaviors
- **Sourced:** every claim references specific principle or institution
- **Honest about lower B2B confidence:** ICE Confidence typically 7-8 rather than 8-9
- **Honest about strategy dependencies:** Example 6 shows how to mark a category as strategy-dependent rather than forcing recommendation
- **Authenticity restraint:** customer-logo recommendations note "permission documented"
- **Test-ready:** hypothesis follows "If X, then Y, because Z"; primary metric is B2B-specific
- **Concrete copy:** exact headline alternatives, exact trust-strip elements, exact CTA copy
- **Dutch translations natural:** when output is Dutch, "trade programma", "Logo wall", "case studies" used appropriately; "huren" never used

Always match this standard. If a finding can't reach this standard, either dig deeper or remove it from the audit.
